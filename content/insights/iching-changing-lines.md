## The Short Answer

One cast of the *I Ching* can hold **three** pieces of information:

- **The primary hexagram (本卦)** — the situation as it stands now.
- **The changing lines (动爻)** — the specific positions where the situation is in motion. A line is "changing" when it is at its extreme: old yin or old yang, about to turn into its opposite.
- **The transformed hexagram (变卦 / 之卦)** — what the primary hexagram becomes when every changing line flips. Traditionally read as the direction the situation is moving toward, or its later stage.

If no lines are changing, the cast is **static**: the situation is read as holding steady, and there is no transformed hexagram.

## Lines Are Read Bottom-Up

A hexagram is built from the bottom line upward — line 1 (初) at the bottom, line 6 (上) at the top. When you cast coins, the first throw is line 1 and the sixth throw is line 6. On GoEast's reading pages the hexagram is drawn with line 1 at the bottom and line 6 at the top, and changing-line positions are counted the same way.

This matters because position carries meaning: the lower lines are associated with the beginning or inner side of a situation, the upper lines with its later or outer development. "Changing line 3" is a different signal from "changing line 5" even in the same hexagram.

## How a Changing Line Forms — and How It Makes the Transformed Hexagram

In the traditional three-coin method, three coins are thrown once per line. Each coin counts 2 (tails) or 3 (heads), so every throw sums to 6, 7, 8, or 9:

| Sum | Name | Line drawn | Changing? |
|-----|------|-----------|-----------|
| 6 | old yin (老陰) | broken (yin) | **yes** — turns into yang |
| 7 | young yang (少陽) | solid (yang) | no |
| 8 | young yin (少陰) | broken (yin) | no |
| 9 | old yang (老陽) | solid (yang) | **yes** — turns into yin |

The six throws, bottom-up, draw the **primary hexagram**. Now flip every changing line — 6 becomes a solid line, 9 becomes a broken line — and read the six lines again: that is the **transformed hexagram**.

### A complete, verified example

This is the exact cast used in [GoEast's public example reading](/examples/iching-writing-habit) (the question was about building a consistent writing habit):

- **Primary hexagram: 32, Heng (恆 / Duration).** Its six lines, bottom-up, are **yin–yang–yang–yang–yin–yin**.
- **Changing line: line 3** — the third throw summed to 9 (old yang), a solid line about to turn.
- **Flip line 3** from solid (yang) to broken (yin): the sequence becomes **yin–yang–yin–yang–yin–yin**, which is **hexagram 40, Jie (解 / Deliverance)**.

So the cast is written: **Hexagram 32, changing line 3, transforming to hexagram 40** — one changing line, counted from the bottom, producing one transformed hexagram. You can see both hexagrams rendered side by side, with line 3 highlighted, in [the example reading](/examples/iching-writing-habit), and read the hexagrams themselves on their own pages: [Hexagram 32 · Heng](/iching/heng) and [Hexagram 40 · Jie](/iching/jie).

## No Changing Lines, One, or Several

**No changing lines.** The cast is static. The situation is read through the primary hexagram alone — its judgment (卦辞) and image (象辭), and what they suggest about your question. There is no transformed hexagram.

**One changing line.** The changing line is the focal point of the reading: where the tension lives, and the thread connecting the primary hexagram to the transformed one. Most traditions give a single changing line special weight.

**Multiple changing lines.** Here the traditions genuinely diverge, and it is worth knowing that no single rule is "the" rule:

- One classical school (systematized by Zhu Xi in the *Yixue Qimeng* 易學啟蒙, 1186) assigns different protocols depending on how many lines change — for example, with two changing lines, read the two changing lines' texts, preferring the lower; with none of the special cases, read the primary hexagram's judgment.
- Other readers take only the **uppermost** changing line as the operative one.
- Still others read the situation primarily through the **pair of hexagrams** — the primary's judgment for the present, the transformed's for the direction — without leaning on individual line texts at all.

These are schools of reading, not facts. What they share: changing lines mark movement, and more changing lines mark a more turbulent, faster-moving situation.

## How GoEast Handles Changing Lines

So you know exactly what our readings do and do not do:

- **Casting:** the consultation simulates the three-coin method with the classical probabilities — each of the six lines has a 1/8 chance of old yin (6), 3/8 young yang (7), 3/8 young yin (8), and 1/8 old yang (9). A line is changing exactly when it sums to 6 or 9.
- **What is quoted:** readings show the classical **judgment (卦辞) and image (象辞)** of your primary hexagram — and of the transformed hexagram when there is one — from our structured database, in Chinese with an English rendering. Individual line texts (爻辞) are **not** in our database: when a reading discusses your changing line, it does so as clearly-labeled interpretation ("my reading of line 3 is…"), never as a quotation.
- **Follow-ups stay on the same cast:** when you ask follow-up questions about a reading, the same primary hexagram, changing lines, and transformed hexagram are used throughout — follow-ups never re-cast.
- **The transformation is computed, not imagined:** the transformed hexagram is derived mechanically by flipping the changing lines; the AI never invents or alters lines.
